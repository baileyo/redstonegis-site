Why only one warrant in nine became the veteran's land

From the Revolution to 1855, Congress paid soldiers in land warrants. On the 572,221 patents those warrants produced, the veteran named on the warrant is the patentee about one time in nine. Three times in four the patent carries the name of someone outside the family. The law, the veterans' own circumstances and a busy market in warrants explain most of it.

1 · The law set the pattern

The act of 1812 gave a regular-army soldier 160 acres in one of three military tracts, in Illinois, Missouri and Arkansas, and the patent was made out to the soldier himself. That is why 85% of those patents carry the soldier's name.

Later acts loosened both rules. Mexican War warrants, under the act of 1847, could be assigned to someone else from the start, and the Commissioner of the General Land Office predicted that year that they would fall to speculators [1]. Warrants under the act of 1850 were at first ruled unassignable [1]; an act of 22 March 1852 let veterans and their heirs assign them [5]. By the act of 1855, which gave 160 acres for as little as 14 days' service, the warrantee is the patentee on 2% of patents.

2 · For most veterans a warrant was cash

The big acts came late. By the 1850s a veteran of the War of 1812 was in his sixties, and using a warrant meant claiming public land on the frontier, far from where most veterans lived. Selling it was the practical choice.

Historians who have worked from the warrant files reach the same conclusion as this map. James W. Oberly sampled the warrants issued under the four acts of 1847–55, which granted about 60 million acres. He found that roughly one American family in nine received a warrant and that families usually sold them for cash to third parties [2]; by one summary of his book, fewer than 5% of recipients used the warrant to take up land themselves [3]. Paul W. Gates found it at the land offices: of the first 300 Mexican War warrants located in the Chicago district, fewer than a dozen were located by the soldier [1].

In this data, the four acts of 1847–55 account for 537,139 patents, and 7% of them carry the warrantee's name.

3 · Who the buyers were

Warrants traded almost like currency. Gates describes agents buying soldiers' claims for $10 to $30 apiece after the act of 1847, and the market value of a warrant climbing toward $1.20 by 1854, close to the government's price of $1.25 an acre [1]. A settler could pay for his claim with a second-hand warrant. Lenders also entered land for settlers and carried the debt for a year at a steep charge [1]. In both cases the patent carries the buyer's or the lender's name, so "someone else" covers ordinary farmers as well as speculators.

The names bear that out. The 436,213 patents issued to someone with a different surname are spread over about 171,000 names. Almost half (49%) went to names that appear on fewer than five warrant patents. At the other end, 239 names appear on 100 or more and together hold 13%.

The largest of those include James S. Easley (1,522 patents) and William W. Willingham (1,007). Easley & Willingham, of Halifax County, Virginia, is the firm Robert P. Swierenga reconstructed from its surviving papers: it dealt in warrants, lent to settlers on "time" entries, and by 1865 held about 92,500 acres in Iowa and 38,000 in Missouri [4]. Names are counted as written, so one common name can be several people, and one dealer can appear under several spellings.

4 · What the number does not show

A name on a patent is not a settler. War of 1812 patents were issued in the soldier's name whatever he did next. In the Illinois Military Tract most soldiers or their heirs chose not to move and sold their rights, and one company alone came to hold 900,000 acres [6]. In Arkansas most veterans never lived in the state or claimed their land, and unclaimed land was sold for unpaid taxes [5]. So one in nine is an upper limit on veterans who kept the land, not a count of veterans who farmed it.

Some of it stayed in the family. Another 12% of patents went to someone with the warrantee's surname: widows, children and brothers, along with some unrelated people who share a common surname.

It is not a measure of fraud. There was fraud, including forged discharges and blank assignments [1], but Oberly found little evidence that speculators monopolized the land or systematically cheated veterans [3]. Most sales were what they look like: an old soldier turning a piece of paper into money.

The match is by name. A patent counts as the warrantee's when the two names are the same person, allowing for initials and spelling. The warrantee is sometimes a widow or heir, not the soldier. About 0.4% of records name no warrantee and are left out of the shares.

Where the veteran kept it most. The warrantee share is highest in Arkansas (35%), Alabama (32%), Florida (26%) and Illinois (25%), and under 2% in Kansas, Nebraska and Minnesota. Arkansas and Illinois hold the 1812 tracts. Alabama and Florida are not explained by the 1812 act and deserve a closer look. See it on the map.

Whose name is on the patent?

From the Revolution to 1855, Congress paid soldiers in land warrants. This map follows each warrant to the patent it produced and asks whether the land went to the veteran. For the reasons behind the answer, see Why 1 in 9.

Veterans' bounty land

This page maps the 572,221 patents issued on military bounty-land warrants: land Congress promised to soldiers of the Revolution, the War of 1812, the Mexican War and, under the acts of 1850, 1852 and 1855, almost anyone who had served since 1790. Each GLO record names the warrantee, the soldier (or the widow or heir) the warrant was issued to, alongside the patentee who received the land. Comparing the two names shows whether a veteran's warrant turned into a veteran's farm.

A patent counts as going to the warrantee when the two names are the same person, allowing for a middle initial or a spelling variant. Same surname means the surname matches but the given name does not: usually a widow, child or brother, though two unrelated people with a common surname will land here too. Someone else means a different surname: the warrant was sold or assigned before the land was claimed. About 0.4% of records name no warrantee and are left out of the shares.

The split follows the law. War of 1812 warrants could not be sold and could be located only in military tracts in Illinois, Arkansas and Missouri, so most of those patents carry the soldier's own name, whether or not he ever moved there. Mexican War warrants could be sold from the start, and the act of 1852 made every warrant assignable; land agents and settlers bought them at a discount and located them on any public land open to sale. A patent in a veteran's name shows that he held title, not that he settled the land.

Ohio needed extra work. Much of the state was surveyed before the standard grid, so the patents there name a township and range in an older survey. Each of those surveys was fitted to the counties its patents name: the U.S. Military District as a regular grid of 5-mile townships, the river-based surveys one range at a time. Nearly every township lands within 5 km of its county; a patent whose fitted position falls more than 8 km outside is placed in the county instead. The GLO index files the U.S. Military District patents under the "United Brethren" name of the same 1796 act; all of them are soldiers' warrants.

The 4,775 Virginia Military District patents carry no township, section or county, because that district was surveyed by metes and bounds. The scanned patents do describe the land, usually as lying "on the waters of" a named creek. Those scans are being read one at a time, in random order. So far 300 have been read and 273 named a stream that could be found; each of those is placed on its stream, at a point along it that the patent does not fix, so its cell is right to within the length of the creek and no closer. This is a sample in progress, not the full picture. The patents not yet read are drawn as one shaded area between the Scioto and Little Miami rivers, with a dashed outline that is approximate.

New York's Military Tract was a state grant and never passed through the GLO, so it has its own row below the total in the table and is not counted in it. Three of its 28 townships (Ovid, Hector and Ulysses) are drawn lot by lot from the Backbone Ridge History Group's transcription of the 1825 Balloting Book, which names the soldier who drew each lot and the person the patent was delivered to. A lot delivered to the soldier's relative, heir or administrator counts as same surname. The lots appear from 1791, the year of the ballot, because the transcription gives no patent dates. Zoom in on the Finger Lakes and hover a lot to read both names.

Not shown: the rest of New York's tract and the Revolutionary War grants of other states, such as Pennsylvania's Donation Lands; and the soldiers' homesteads of 1872 and later, which the GLO index files with every other homestead.

Which war

The Which war view colours the same patents by the service behind the warrant. The records never name the war outright. For the early acts the law settles it: the acts of 1790 and 1796 and the refugee acts paid for the Revolution, the act of 1812 for regular-army enlistments in the War of 1812, and the act of 1847 for the Mexican War. The acts of 1850, 1852 and 1855 covered any war since 1790, so for those four patents in five the record gives only a rank and a unit, such as "Captain Lewis Company Virginia Militia". The map sorts them by what the unit says: militia, volunteers, or U.S. regulars, navy and marines.

Most of the militia patents will be War of 1812 service, because that war called out far more militia than any other before 1855, but the record does not say so, and the same rows hold men from the Indian wars. Volunteer companies include Cherokee, Seminole and New Mexico units named in the records. About 740 patents under the act of 1855 have "Revolutionary War" written in the unit and are counted with the Revolution. The New York lots also count as Revolutionary War when that layer is on.

Reading it carefully

Year is the patent's signature date. A few thousand twentieth-century dates are reissues of older patents.

Location comes from each patent's township, range and section, binned to a 0.1° grid (about 11 km north–south). A patent that spans two cells is counted in both, so map totals run slightly above the patent counts in the table. About 1.2% of warrant patents could not be placed.
