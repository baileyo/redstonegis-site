About this map

Until 1909 a federal patent passed the land whole, surface and minerals together; land known to hold minerals was kept out of farm entry and sold under the mining and coal laws. From 1909 Congress began to keep what lay underneath, and from 1920 it leased those minerals. The result is split estate: private or state surface over minerals the United States still owns.

Each square is a tenth of a degree. It is coloured for full title unless at least 5% of the acres patented in it by the selected year carry a mineral reservation; then it takes the colour of the largest kind of reservation, in three strengths (5–20%, 20–50%, over 50%). Click a legend entry to switch a kind off.

Source: the Bureau of Land Management's General Land Office records for the 30 public-land states, 5.94 million patents. The index has a yes-or-no field, "Subsurface Reserved". It does not say which minerals or under which law, so the kinds here are read from each patent's authority and date. "Coal or named minerals" is a presumption: the patent itself says whether coal, or oil, gas, phosphate and potash, were kept. Flags on patents dated before 1909 are shown as not explained.

Not shown: minerals a private seller kept in a later deed, which sit in county records; the 13 original states, Texas and the other state-land states; mineral rights sold back to surface owners since 1976; and about 10,500 patents with no usable date. Acres are the index acreage, placed at the patent's mapped locations, so a large grant can exceed the square it sits in. The law notes are from general knowledge and have not been checked against the statute texts.

Alaska is an inset at about a third of its true size.
