About this map

This page shows land held for conservation, each unit switched on in the year it was established and drawn at its present extent. It uses the same tenth-of-a-degree cells as the patent map. A cell takes the colour of whichever holder has most of its protected land, and is stronger the more of the cell is protected.

What counts as protected. Land in the U.S. Geological Survey's Protected Areas Database of the United States 4.1 with a protection status of 1, 2 or 3: kept in natural cover, from strict reserves to multiple-use land such as most national forests and Bureau of Land Management land. Status 4 land (no known mandate against conversion) is left out; that removes most military land, most local parks and much state trust land, though the database gives status 3 to trust land in some states, Montana, Idaho and Colorado among them. Indian reservations are not on this page.

Federal, state, local and private conservation land are owned outright by the holder. Private conservation land belongs to land trusts and other private owners. Conservation easements are private land whose development rights are held by an agency or a land trust; they are counted once, as easements, whoever holds them.

Dates. The database's date is the year a unit received its present name and designation, which can be later than the year the land was first protected. Federal units without one take the dates of the Federal Lands Time Series page (the national forest table and its hand-entered parks). Bureau of Land Management public land has no establishment date; it is switched on in 1976, when the Federal Land Policy and Management Act made keeping the public lands the stated policy, which is why federal land roughly doubles that year. More than half of the state, local and easement acreage has no date at all. That land is phased in on the pattern of the dated land of the same kind in the same state, so the totals grow at a believable pace, but the year for any one such unit is a placeholder and the page says "date not recorded" for it.

Limits. Units appear at today's extent, so land protected and later released is absent and a unit that grew over time appears all at once. Designations laid over federal land (wilderness, wild rivers, monuments inside forests) are not shown separately. Records of 2,000 acres or more are drawn from their outlines; smaller ones are counted in the cell where their centre falls. Easement records are incomplete: the national easement database stopped being updated in January 2025, and some holders never shared their records. Acreages are measured from the cells and will not match agency tables exactly. Alaska and Hawaii are insets, Alaska at about a third of its true size.
