Thirty-two states, thirty-two land rushes

Every public-land state was patented on its own clock. Ohio and Indiana were largely in private hands before the Civil War; the Dakotas, Montana and Washington boomed after 1900; Alaska's conveyances run to the present. Texas, which kept its public land when it joined the Union, granted it through its own General Land Office. North Carolina, one of the original thirteen, granted its land from 1663 on, including what became Tennessee. Select a state to see it on the map.

The laws behind the curve

    About this map

    Between 1785 and the late twentieth century the federal government transferred more than a billion acres of public domain to private owners, one land patent at a time. The Bureau of Land Management's General Land Office records hold the surviving patents. This site maps them.

    Each patent describes its land by the Public Land Survey System: meridian, township, range and section. Placing those descriptions on the PLSS grid turns a records archive into a map of when each part of the country passed into private hands.

    How it was built

    1. Patent records for each state were downloaded from BLM's GLO Records search as paged JSON responses. The search returns at most about 100,000 records per query, so glo_backfill.py split every larger state into date ranges (and, for undated New Mexico patents, counties) below that limit.

    2. build_glo_patents_gis.py parsed every legal description, resolved each meridian code against BLM's LR2000 meridian table, and placed each location at its PLSS section centroid, falling back to the township centroid where no section was given. The output is one GeoPackage per state: a patent_locations point layer joined one-to-many to a patent_attributes table.

    3. apply_glo_location_fix.py corrected those positions, because an idealized 6-mile grid drifts away from the real survey: up to 60 km by western Wyoming and about 35 km across North Dakota. In 17 western states each section is now placed inside its actual township polygon from the BLM survey (via the Late Homesteading replication data). Elsewhere a smooth correction per meridian moves points so they fall inside the county each patent names; tested against the western townships, it cut North Dakota's median error from 35 km to 8 km. About 5,800 locations (0.24%) that still landed more than 20 km outside their state, usually from a mis-coded meridian, are left off the map. Alaska keeps the grid positions.

    4. aggregate_glo_timeslider.py binned every located patent into a 0.1° grid (about 11 km north–south) by year and entry type. A patent is counted once per cell, so one that spans two cells appears in both.

    5. aggregate_glo_states.py counted distinct patents per state by year and type for the States and Timeline pages.

    Surname origins

    The Surname Origin tab groups patents by the presumed language or region of origin of the patentee's surname. It describes surnames, not people: a surname follows the paternal line, often several generations back, many were anglicized on arrival, and most nineteenth-century patentees were born in the United States. Share of one origin colours each cell by the share of its patents in the chosen group; Leading origin colours it by the most common group among those switched on, so switching off English/Welsh shows the next most common.

    Each patent is classified from the first patentee listed. Companies, railroads, states, counties, churches and tribes are set aside as organizations. Patents issued under Indian allotment, trust and fee-patent authorities are shown as Native allottee, and Spanish and Mexican grants and private land claims as Spanish/Mexican/French-era claim, because there the patent authority says more than the surname. Every other surname was matched, in order, against a hand review of the most frequent GLO surnames, lexicons of Irish, Scottish, Dutch, Scandinavian, German and Welsh names, a few unambiguous endings and particles (-sson, -sen, -ski, -ini, -eau, O', Van), and finally a character-pattern model trained on about 62,000 labelled surnames. Names the model could not place with at least 40% confidence are Uncertain (about 5% of patents).

    Treat the splits with care. English/Welsh is over-counted, since anglicized German, Irish and Dutch names such as Miller or Carpenter fall there. English, Scottish and Irish, and German and Dutch, are the least reliable distinctions. In a weighted spot check about 94% of confident labels were right; for rare surnames the model is right roughly two times in three. A cell where 30% of patentees carry German surnames is a reliable signal of German settlement; a single patent in a cell is not.

    Texas

    Texas kept its public land when it joined the Union in 1845, so it has no BLM patents and no township-and-range survey. Every original title runs through the Texas General Land Office instead, through Spanish and Mexican titles, Republic headrights, military bounties and donations, railroad and other land scrip, preemption homesteads, and the sale of school and university lands. Texas parcels are identified by county and abstract number rather than by section.

    The 666,990 records in the Texas GLO's online Land Grant Database were read in November 2026 and joined by county and abstract number to the Railroad Commission of Texas's original land survey polygons. 97.5% of polygons with an abstract number found a GLO record, and on 94.8% of those the Railroad Commission's survey name agrees with the GLO grantee. Each grant is placed at a point inside its survey, so a large grant counts in one cell rather than every cell it covers.

    Year is the patent date. Records with no patent date (316,958, mostly mineral files, clerk returns, court-of-claims papers and sales never carried to patent) are not on the map, nor are 54,526 dated records that have no abstract number (26,758) or whose survey has no polygon (27,768), mostly small subdivided tracts. Texas grant classes are grouped into the same entry types as the federal patents: preemption as homestead; school and university land sales as cash sale; land scrip (including railroad certificates), military bounties and donations, and Confederate scrip as warrant and scrip; headrights, Spanish and Mexican titles, clerk returns and Peters Colony grants as grants and claims; mineral files as mineral. Grants filed as first-class headrights but larger than a league and a labor (4,605.5 acres) are Spanish and Mexican grants later confirmed by the State and are counted as such. The Surname Origin tab classifies Texas patents by the original grantee.

    North Carolina

    North Carolina was never federal public domain. Its land passed into private hands by grants from the Lords Proprietors and the Crown until the Revolution, and from the State after 1777. There was no township-and-range survey: each tract was described by metes and bounds, usually starting from a stream or a neighbour's line. The State also granted land in what became Tennessee, including the bounty land promised to its Continental Line soldiers, and those grants are counted here under North Carolina.

    The 216,054 land grant files catalogued by the State Archives of North Carolina (Secretary of State, Land Office: land warrants, plats of survey and related records) were read in November 2026. Year is the date the grant was issued, or the date of entry where no issue date is recorded. Because these grants begin in 1663, the timeline on this site now starts there; federal patents still begin in 1785.

    These records have no surveyed positions, so North Carolina is mapped more coarsely than the other states. Each grant is first placed in its county as that county's boundaries stood on the grant date. Where the description names a stream that can be found in that county, the grant is drawn in the cell at the middle of the stream's course through the county (105,307 grants). The rest (108,070) are spread evenly over the cells of their county. A cell's count in North Carolina therefore shows roughly where granting happened, not where a particular tract lies, and each grant in a cell's list says which kind of placement it has.

    Not on the map: 1,043 files with no usable date, and 1,634 dated files filed under a Tennessee district or with no county given. Grants issued on military bounty warrants are counted as warrant and scrip; all other grants as grants and claims. The Surname Origin tab classifies each grant by its first-named grantee.

    Reading it carefully

    Year is the patent's signature date, or its search date where no signature date was recorded. That date marks when title passed, which can lag settlement by five years or more under the Homestead Act.

    Entry types are grouped from the GLO "authority" field. Homestead covers original, enlarged and stock-raising entries; cash sale includes credit sales; warrant and scrip covers military bounty warrants and scrip; grants and claims covers state grants, railroad and Indian patents, and private land claims. Non-patent records are BLM land-status entries (CDI, survey plats and LSR serial records) that sit alongside patents in the GLO database.

    Completeness: the site holds 5.9 million dated patents, 99.5% or more of what the GLO search reports for each state except Alaska (86%) and Ohio (96%). An earlier download had stopped at the search's 100,000-record limit in 25 states; those gaps were filled in November 2026, along with the land-status (CDI) and survey records, which are now 99.8% complete or better.

    What's missing: records without any usable date (about 190,000 location rows, concentrated in Alaska and New Mexico), locations under unspecified meridians, and some special surveys that do not fit the rectangular grid. The original thirteen states were never federal public domain and have no GLO patents. Texas wasn't federal land either, but it kept and granted its own; see Texas above. North Carolina is the first of the thirteen to be added, from its own state records; see North Carolina above.
