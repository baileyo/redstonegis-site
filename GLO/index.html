Forty-four states, forty-four land rushes

Every public-land state was patented on its own clock. Ohio and Indiana were largely in private hands before the Civil War; the Dakotas, Montana and Washington boomed after 1900; Alaska's conveyances run to the present. Texas, which kept its public land when it joined the Union, granted it through its own General Land Office. North Carolina, one of the original thirteen, granted its land from 1663 on, including what became Tennessee; those grants are shown under Tennessee. Maryland's patents, which begin in 1634, are shown by county. Vermont's land was granted a whole town at a time, and its town charters are shown by town. Rhode Island never had a land office; its proprietors' purchases and colony grants are shown town by town. Connecticut's land was also granted a town at a time; its 62 original towns are shown, each dated to the year it was established or its land was granted. Virginia's patents and grants, which begin in 1623, are shown by county as each county stood at the time; the land Virginia granted in what are now West Virginia and Kentucky is shown under those states. Georgia granted its eastern third by headright, shown by county or stream, and gave out the rest in eight land lotteries between 1805 and 1833, shown lot by lot. New Hampshire's townships were granted by four different authorities; 168 first grants are shown. Massachusetts is shown by its 141 original towns and township grants, from Plymouth in 1620. Maine's land was sold and granted by Massachusetts until 1820, usually a township at a time; the townships whose year is known are shown. Select a state to see it on the map.

The laws behind the curve

    About this map

    Between 1785 and the late twentieth century the federal government transferred more than a billion acres of public domain to private owners, one land patent at a time. The Bureau of Land Management's General Land Office records hold the surviving patents. This site maps them.

    Each patent describes its land by the Public Land Survey System: meridian, township, range and section. Placing those descriptions on the PLSS grid turns a records archive into a map of when each part of the country passed into private hands.

    How it was built

    1. Patent records for each state were downloaded from BLM's GLO Records search as paged JSON responses. The search returns at most about 100,000 records per query, so glo_backfill.py split every larger state into date ranges (and, for undated New Mexico patents, counties) below that limit.

    2. build_glo_patents_gis.py parsed every legal description, resolved each meridian code against BLM's LR2000 meridian table, and placed each location at its PLSS section centroid, falling back to the township centroid where no section was given. The output is one GeoPackage per state: a patent_locations point layer joined one-to-many to a patent_attributes table.

    3. apply_glo_location_fix.py corrected those positions, because an idealized 6-mile grid drifts away from the real survey: up to 60 km by western Wyoming and about 35 km across North Dakota. In 17 western states each section is now placed inside its actual township polygon from the BLM survey (via the Late Homesteading replication data). Elsewhere a smooth correction per meridian moves points so they fall inside the county each patent names; tested against the western townships, it cut North Dakota's median error from 35 km to 8 km. About 5,800 locations (0.24%) that still landed more than 20 km outside their state, usually from a mis-coded meridian, are left off the map. Alaska keeps the grid positions.

    4. aggregate_glo_timeslider.py binned every located patent into a 0.1° grid (about 11 km north–south) by year and entry type. A patent is counted once per cell, so one that spans two cells appears in both.

    5. aggregate_glo_states.py counted distinct patents per state by year and type for the States and Timeline pages.

    Surname origins

    The Surname Origin tab groups patents by the presumed language or region of origin of the patentee's surname. It describes surnames, not people: a surname follows the paternal line, often several generations back, many were anglicized on arrival, and most nineteenth-century patentees were born in the United States. Share of one origin colours each cell by the share of its patents in the chosen group; Leading origin colours it by the most common group among those switched on, so switching off English/Welsh shows the next most common.

    Each patent is classified from the first patentee listed. Companies, railroads, states, counties, churches and tribes are set aside as organizations. Patents issued under Indian allotment, trust and fee-patent authorities are shown as Native allottee, and Spanish and Mexican grants and private land claims as Spanish/Mexican/French-era claim, because there the patent authority says more than the surname. Every other surname was matched, in order, against a hand review of the most frequent GLO surnames, lexicons of Irish, Scottish, Dutch, Scandinavian, German and Welsh names, a few unambiguous endings and particles (-sson, -sen, -ski, -ini, -eau, O', Van), and finally a character-pattern model trained on about 62,000 labelled surnames. Names the model could not place with at least 40% confidence are Uncertain (about 5% of patents).

    Treat the splits with care. English/Welsh is over-counted, since anglicized German, Irish and Dutch names such as Miller or Carpenter fall there. English, Scottish and Irish, and German and Dutch, are the least reliable distinctions. In a weighted spot check about 94% of confident labels were right; for rare surnames the model is right roughly two times in three. A cell where 30% of patentees carry German surnames is a reliable signal of German settlement; a single patent in a cell is not.

    Texas

    Texas kept its public land when it joined the Union in 1845, so it has no BLM patents and no township-and-range survey. Every original title runs through the Texas General Land Office instead, through Spanish and Mexican titles, Republic headrights, military bounties and donations, railroad and other land scrip, preemption homesteads, and the sale of school and university lands. Texas parcels are identified by county and abstract number rather than by section.

    The 666,990 records in the Texas GLO's online Land Grant Database were read in November 2026 and joined by county and abstract number to the Railroad Commission of Texas's original land survey polygons. 97.5% of polygons with an abstract number found a GLO record, and on 94.8% of those the Railroad Commission's survey name agrees with the GLO grantee. Each grant is placed at a point inside its survey, so a large grant counts in one cell rather than every cell it covers.

    Year is the patent date. Records with no patent date (316,958, mostly mineral files, clerk returns, court-of-claims papers and sales never carried to patent) are not on the map, nor are 54,526 dated records that have no abstract number (26,758) or whose survey has no polygon (27,768), mostly small subdivided tracts. Texas grant classes are grouped into the same entry types as the federal patents: preemption as homestead; school and university land sales as cash sale; land scrip (including railroad certificates), military bounties and donations, and Confederate scrip as warrant and scrip; headrights, Spanish and Mexican titles, clerk returns and Peters Colony grants as grants and claims; mineral files as mineral. Grants filed as first-class headrights but larger than a league and a labor (4,605.5 acres) are Spanish and Mexican grants later confirmed by the State and are counted as such. The Surname Origin tab classifies Texas patents by the original grantee.

    North Carolina

    North Carolina was never federal public domain. Its land passed into private hands by grants from the Lords Proprietors and the Crown until the Revolution, and from the State after 1777. There was no township-and-range survey: each tract was described by metes and bounds, usually starting from a stream or a neighbour's line. The State also granted land in what became Tennessee, including the bounty land promised to its Continental Line soldiers, and those grants are counted here under Tennessee, the state where the land lies today, though North Carolina issued them all: 12,415 grants (9.0 million acres), 10,592 of them on the map. North Carolina's own count is the 203,639 grants for land inside the present state. Virginia's grants in what are now West Virginia and Kentucky are treated the same way; see Virginia, West Virginia and Kentucky below. Each Tennessee-land grant also carries a phase tag: North Carolina before the cession (to 1 April 1790), the Southwest Territory years (2 April 1790 to 31 May 1796), or the State of Tennessee. The tag does not change where a grant is counted; the States table lists the grants in each phase under Tennessee.

    The federal public-land states carry the same kind of tag. Each patent is matched to the state or territory that governed its land on the day it was signed: Northwest Territory, Indiana Territory, Michigan Territory, Dakota Territory and so on, following the dated state and territory outlines of the Newberry Library's Atlas of Historical County Boundaries. Of 5,934,387 dated federal patents, 547,042 (9.2%) were signed before the land was part of a state. The States table gives each state's patents by phase, and a patent signed before statehood shows its territory in a cell's list. A patent with no mapped position takes its state's usual sequence. Phases with a handful of patents usually mean a misrecorded date. Counts stay under the present state.

    The 216,054 land grant files catalogued by the State Archives of North Carolina (Secretary of State, Land Office: land warrants, plats of survey and related records) were read in November 2026. Year is the date the grant was issued, or the date of entry where no issue date is recorded. Because these grants begin in 1663, the timeline was extended back for them; federal patents still begin in 1785.

    These records have no surveyed positions, so North Carolina is mapped more coarsely than the other states. Each grant is first placed in its county as that county's boundaries stood on the grant date. Where the description names a stream that can be found in that county, the grant is drawn in the cell at the middle of the stream's course through the county (105,307 grants). The rest (108,070) are spread evenly over the cells of their county. A cell's count in North Carolina therefore shows roughly where granting happened, not where a particular tract lies, and each grant in a cell's list says which kind of placement it has.

    Not on the map: 1,043 files with no usable date, and 1,634 dated files filed under a Tennessee district or with no county given. Grants issued on military bounty warrants are counted as warrant and scrip; all other grants as grants and claims. The Surname Origin tab classifies each grant by its first-named grantee.

    Maryland

    Maryland was never federal public domain. From 1634 its land was granted by the Lords Baltimore as proprietors, and after the Revolution by the State, through a Land Office that still exists. A tract was claimed by warrant, laid out in a certificate of survey, and given a name; the patent conveyed it. There was no township-and-range survey.

    The 48,873 patents shown here come from the county tract indexes of the Maryland State Archives (109,353 index entries in 23 county series, read in November 2026). Each patent entry gives the tract name, its acreage, the year and the Patent Record volume and page. Entries for certificates of survey are not counted, because the same tract appears again as a patent, and neither are the 11,823 certificates that were never patented. Because these patents begin in 1634, the timeline was extended back for them.

    Maryland is mapped at county level only. The index records the county where the land lies today, not a position, so each patent is assigned to one of the cells of that county, spread evenly; Baltimore City's tracts are indexed under Baltimore County. A cell's count shows how much was patented in the county, not where a tract lies. The index does not name the patentee, so Maryland has no entries in the name search and is not in the Surname Origin view.

    Two cautions. Resurveys are counted: when a tract was resurveyed and patented again, often enlarged or combined with its neighbours, that is a second patent of some of the same land. Maryland's patent count therefore overstates the land first granted, and no acreage total is shown for the state: the stated acres of these patents sum to 10.7 million, in a state of about 6.2 million. And the split into proprietary and state patents is by year alone. Not on the map: 195 patent entries with no usable year. All Maryland patents are counted as grants and claims.

    Vermont

    Vermont was never federal public domain, and it never had a land office that patented tracts to individuals. Its land was granted a town at a time. A charter gave a whole township, nominally six miles square, to a list of proprietors, usually sixty or so, with shares set aside for a church, a school and a first minister. The proprietors then surveyed the town into lots and drew for them. A person named on a charter was a shareholder, often a speculator who never came; the settler's title came later, from the proprietors' division or by deed.

    Three governments granted the same ground. New Hampshire's governor, Benning Wentworth, chartered towns west of the Connecticut River from 1749 to 1764. New York, which also claimed the territory, issued its own patents from 1765, many of them laid over New Hampshire towns. Vermont, independent from 1777, chartered the remaining land in the north and centre of the state from 1779.

    The 235 entries shown here are town charters: 129 from New Hampshire, 102 from Vermont, and 4 towns that begin with a New York patent. Each is one record, so Vermont's count is of towns granted, not of people who received land, and it is not comparable with the patent counts of other states. Dates, acreages and the first-named grantee come from the historical notes in State Papers of Vermont, vol. II (1922). For some Vermont towns the date is the legislature's grant, with the charter issued later.

    Not counted: about a hundred New York patents that overlapped towns already granted or were later confirmed, regrants of the same town, and gores and small grants. No acreage total is shown for the state, because charter acreages are nominal figures (23,040 acres for six miles square), not surveyed areas. Each charter is placed in the cell of its present-day town; towns later divided or renamed are listed under the charter they came from. The first-named grantees are not in the name search or the Surname Origin view. All Vermont charters are counted as grants and claims.

    Rhode Island

    Rhode Island was never federal public domain, and the colony never ran a land office. There is no patent series. Land left Native hands by deed: a sachem conveyed a tract to a company of purchasers, the colony confirmed it under the 1663 charter, and the company divided the tract among its shareholders by drawn lots, recording the divisions in its own books. The unit of first title is therefore the purchase, not the farm.

    Of the 84 entries shown here, 49 are those purchases, divided along present-day town lines so that each town's share of a purchase is its own entry: 22 purchases and grants in 45 town pieces, and 4 claims that lay inside a larger purchase. The other 35 are the colony's own deeds for the Narragansett vacant lands, described below. They are purchases by Indian deed, from Providence in 1637 to the Devil's Foot purchase of 1672; acts of the colony itself, the 1677 East Greenwich grant of a hundred acres each to forty-eight named men and the sales of the Narragansett "vacant lands" from 1709; the land the Narragansett sachem Ninigret kept back in 1709, later sold off under committees of the General Assembly; Block Island; and tracts in the eastern towns, which were granted under Plymouth Colony and Massachusetts and came to Rhode Island in 1747 and 1862; they are dated by the original purchase under Plymouth, not by the transfer. A town's entry repeats the date and first holders of the purchase it belonged to; it is not a separate grant, and most of these towns were set off long after the purchase. Rhode Island's count is therefore mostly of tracts and towns, not of people who received land, and it is not comparable with the patent counts of other states.

    The 45 town pieces are mapped areas that together cover the state, 661,684 acres; the 4 inside claims carry no acreage. Outlines are present-day town lines, with 6 towns split where the Pettaquamscutt, Boston Neck, Stanton, Hall and Narragansett-reserve lines cross them, cut from their recorded bounds, so an entry's acreage is the area drawn, not a figure from a deed. Purchases overlapped and were disputed for decades, above all in the Narragansett country, which Rhode Island, Connecticut and a Massachusetts company all claimed; the map shows one holder for each piece of ground.

    The vacant lands are the one place where Rhode Island sold land the way a land office would. In 1709 the General Assembly named a committee to sell the country west of the older Narragansett purchases, and between May 1709 and 1712 it gave 35 deeds to 187 named grantees, some to one man and some to companies of twenty or more. Each deed is an entry here, dated to the day, listing every grantee, and all of them are in the name search. A deed is placed in the cell of the town its bounds point to, which is as close as the printed list allows; 33 of the deeds state their acreage, 78,986 acres together. That land lies inside the vacant-land and West Greenwich town entries, so the deeds' acres are shown but not added to the state's total, and the West Greenwich purchase appears both as a town entry and as its deed. The names are read from the printed list in Potter and have not been checked against the Land Evidences.

    Other dates are to the year. The years for Pawtuxet, Bristol and the eastern purchases under Plymouth (Warren with Barrington, Little Compton, Cumberland and East Providence) are taken from secondary sources rather than the deeds and are marked; the Pocasset (Tiverton) and Westconnaug years are not yet checked. Sources are John Hutchins Cady's Rhode Island Boundaries, 1636–1936, Elisha R. Potter's Early History of Narragansett (1835) and the Records of the Colony of Rhode Island. Apart from the vacant-land grantees, the first holders are not in the name search, and Rhode Island is not in the Surname Origin view. All Rhode Island entries are counted as grants and claims.

    One town is also drawn in detail. Zoomed in on East Greenwich, the map shows the farms of the first and second divisions as William Hall platted them in 1716, traced from the copy printed by William Davis Miller in 1937: 100 farms and the Great Bog Meadow. Eighty carry the holder's name, eighteen only the initials written on the plat, and two nothing. These are the people holding each farm in 1716, not the forty-eight grantees of 1677, and the outlines are fitted to present-day roads and good only to a few hundred metres. The farms are a picture laid over the town's entry: they add nothing to the counts or the acreage, and their holders can be found through the name search but are not in the Surname Origin view.

    Virginia, West Virginia and Kentucky

    Virginia was never federal public domain. Its land was patented by the colony's Secretary from 1623 to 1774, and granted by the Commonwealth's Land Office from 1779. The Northern Neck, between the Rappahannock and the Potomac, was granted separately by the Fairfax proprietors until the 1780s and then by the Commonwealth in its own series of books. There was no township-and-range survey: each tract was described by metes and bounds, usually from a stream or a neighbour's line. Virginia also granted the land that became Kentucky, until Kentucky became a state in 1792, and West Virginia, until it separated in 1863.

    The 146,465 patents and grants shown here are the entries of the Land Office index in the catalog of the Library of Virginia, read in November 2026: one entry for each patent or grant recorded in the Land Office books, with the grantee, date, county, acreage and a short description. They are shown under the state where the land lies today: 91,131 in Virginia, 45,826 in West Virginia and 9,508 in Kentucky, although Virginia issued them all. Because these patents begin in 1623, the timeline was extended back for them; federal patents still begin in 1785.

    These records have no surveyed positions. Each is placed in its county as that county's boundaries stood on the grant date, and spread evenly over the cells of that outline that lie in its present state. Early counties were very large: Augusta County in 1750 covered most of western Virginia, so its patents are spread over hundreds of cells, while by 1800 it was a few dozen. A cell's count shows roughly where granting happened, not where a tract lies. The present state is taken from the Library's catalog headings, which follow the county named in the grant: a patent in old Augusta County is shown in Virginia even where the land itself is now across the line in West Virginia.

    Three cautions. No acreage total is shown for these states: the stated acres sum to 41 million for Virginia, a state of about 25 million acres, and 26 million for West Virginia, a state of about 15 million, because Virginia granted the same ground more than once, and many patents re-granted or enlarged earlier ones. The index is not quite complete: about one entry in a hundred could not be read from the catalog. And a few hundred dates in the catalog are plainly wrong; a Land Office grant dated before 1779 is left off the timeline. Not on the map: 150 entries with no usable date and 2,831 dated entries with no county, most of them Kentucky grants located only in the military district. Also left out are 13,554 modern grants of escheated lots, dated 1950 or later and most of them from 1980 to 2000, which are not original title. All entries are counted as grants and claims. Grantees are in the name search, and the Surname Origin tab classifies each entry by its first-named grantee.

    Connecticut

    Connecticut was never federal public domain, and it never had a land office that patented tracts to individuals. Land left the colony's hands a town at a time. The General Court granted or recognised a town-sized tract, and the town's proprietors divided it among themselves; every later transfer was recorded by the town clerk. There is no colony-wide list of grantees.

    The 62 entries shown here are the original towns from which today's 169 descend. Each later town was set off from an older one, and is listed under the original town it traces back to. 3 are the river towns of the 1630s. 4 began under New Haven Colony and one under Saybrook Colony, both later absorbed by Connecticut. 3 were granted by Massachusetts Bay and annexed to Connecticut in 1749. 7 north-western townships were sold in shares at auction in 1737–38, and 8 more in the north-west, Litchfield first, went to the proprietors of Hartford and Windsor. The auction townships, the Hartford and Windsor lands, the Massachusetts towns and Voluntown have been checked against the colony records; the other classes rest on general histories.

    The year is the one the Connecticut State Library gives for the town's establishment. For the earliest towns that is when the town was settled or named, not a dated grant. Fifteen towns are dated earlier than the Library's year, by the act that put the land in private hands: the seven auction townships at 1737, when the General Assembly ordered them sold; the Hartford and Windsor tracts at 1732 and 1733, when their partition was confirmed and the tracts named; and Voluntown at 1700, when the volunteers' tract was bounded. The entry for each also gives the Library's year for the town. Each entry is one record, so Connecticut's count is of towns, not of people who received land, and it is not comparable with the patent counts of other states. The acreage is the present land area of the towns that descend from each original town, 3,099,193 acres in all. A town formed from more than one older town is counted under the first one the Library names.

    Each entry is placed in a cell inside its original town. Connecticut is not in the name search or the Surname Origin view. All entries are counted as grants and claims. The half-million acres in Ohio that Connecticut granted in 1792 to people burned out in the Revolution are on a separate page, The Fire Lands.

    Georgia

    Georgia kept its own land and gave it out under two systems. East of roughly the Oconee River, and along the coast, land was granted by headright from the colony's founding: a settler chose a tract, the county surveyor ran it by metes and bounds, and the governor signed the grant. Revolutionary bounty grants used the same procedure. West and north of that line, as the Creek and Cherokee were forced to cede their land between 1802 and 1835, the state surveyed each cession into districts and square numbered land lots before anyone held title, and then drew names for the lots in eight lotteries from 1805 to 1833.

    The headright side is shown from the Georgia Archives' index to the Surveyor General's recorded plat books: 37,973 plats, 1735 to 1866. It is an index of recorded plats, not of every grant, and it ends before the last headright grants of 1909. A plat is placed on the stream the index names where that stream can be found in the county as it stood that year, and otherwise somewhere in that county; plats filed only under "Parishes" are spread over the colonial parishes together. More than a third date from 1784 to 1787, when Washington and Franklin counties were opened. Two hundred plats of 20,000 acres or more, nearly all from the 1790s, are surveys far larger than a headright allowed; they are shown but left out of the state's acreage.

    The lottery side is shown lot by lot: 141,840 land lots from the state's own land lot layer, each dated by the year of its lottery. A fortunate drawer had to pay a fee to take out the grant, and lots not taken up reverted to the state and were sold, so the lottery year is when a lot was drawn, not when it was granted. Drawers are named for the 1832 Cherokee land lottery only (18,104 lots), read by machine from James F. Smith's list of 1838 and not proofread; each shows the drawer's home county and whether the lot had been granted by 1838. The other lotteries, including the 1832 gold lottery, show the lot without a name. A drawer is the person who won a lot, often not the person who settled it.

    Georgia's acreage adds the headright plats under 20,000 acres to the mapped area of the lottery lots. Headright surveys overlapped and were sometimes repeated, so the total runs above the state's area. All Georgia entries are counted as grants and claims. Headright grantees and 1832 drawers are in the name search; Georgia is not yet in the Surname Origin view.

    New Hampshire

    New Hampshire was never federal public domain, and it never had a land office that patented tracts to individuals. Its land was granted a township at a time to a body of proprietors, who surveyed it into lots and drew for them. What makes New Hampshire unusual is that several authorities made those grants, sometimes for the same ground.

    The 168 entries shown here are the first dated grant of each township or tract, covering 230 of the state's 259 present towns, cities and unincorporated places. 88 were granted by the provincial governor and council, most of them by Benning and John Wentworth between 1749 and 1775. 35 were granted by Massachusetts Bay, which claimed the Merrimack valley and the south-west until the boundary was settled in 1741. 26 came from the Masonian Proprietors, twelve Portsmouth men who bought John Tufton Mason's claim in 1746 and granted townships within sixty miles of the sea. 17 are sales and grants by the State of New Hampshire in the White Mountains and the north, 1805 to 1855. 2 trace to patents of the Council for New England.

    Each entry is one record, so New Hampshire's count is of grants, not of people who received land, and it is not comparable with the patent counts of other states. A town that was later set off from a granted township is listed under that grant and is not a record of its own. Regrants of the same township, which were common when the first grantees failed to settle, are not counted; where a Massachusetts township was later regranted by the Masonian Proprietors or the province, the earlier grant is the one shown. The acreage is the present land area of the towns in each grant, 5,112,176 acres in all, not the acreage named in the charters.

    Not shown: 29 places with no dated grant in the sources read so far. Most are the oldest seacoast towns, Dover and Exeter and the towns set off from them, which were settled before any township grant was made. Dates, grantees and the granting authority come from the editor's notes and charter texts in the New Hampshire State Papers, read by machine from scanned text; for about nine grants in ten the authority is named in the note or was confirmed from the charter text, and the rest follow the section of the volume they are printed in. Seven of the northern tracts are dated from secondary sources only and are marked. Each entry is placed in a cell inside its town. New Hampshire is not in the name search or the Surname Origin view. All entries are counted as grants and claims. Three townships, Stoddard, Mason and Henniker, are drawn lot by lot from their proprietors' draw lists on a separate page, New Hampshire proprietors' lots.

    Massachusetts

    Massachusetts was never federal public domain and never patented land to individuals from a land office. The General Court granted or recognised land a town at a time. The town or its proprietors then divided it among the inhabitants, and those divisions are in town and proprietors' records, not in any state series.

    The 141 entries shown here are the root towns of the state's 351 present cities and towns: every town that was not set off from an older one. 48 date from the Massachusetts Bay Colony and 17 from Plymouth Colony, before the two were joined in 1692. 65 were granted or established under the Province, 1692 to 1780, most of them in Worcester County and the west. Eight were first organised under the Commonwealth. Three, on Martha's Vineyard and Nantucket, were under New York until 1692.

    60 are colony towns, whose first entry in the records is an order naming the town or setting its bounds. The other 81 began as a plantation, a numbered township or a grant to named people. Eight of those were granted to veterans or their heirs: the Narragansett townships for King Philip's War, the Canada townships for the expedition of 1690, and two others. Ten were sold at auction by the Province on 2 June 1762.

    61 entries are dated by a grant, sale or confirmation read in the records of the two colonies, the Province resolves or the House journals. The other 80 are dated by the year the town was established, which for a colony town is as close to a grant as the records come, and for a later plantation means no grant date was found. Where only a later confirmation was read, or the link between a grant and the present town is inferred, the entry says so.

    Each entry is one record, so the count is of towns, not of people who received land, and it is not comparable with the patent counts of other states. A town set off from an older town is listed under its root town; a town made from parts of several towns is listed under the first one named. The acreage is the present land area of the towns in each entry, 4,992,599 acres in all. Maine, which Massachusetts governed until 1820, is not included here. Each entry is placed in a cell inside its town. Massachusetts is not in the name search or the Surname Origin view. All entries are counted as grants and claims.

    Maine

    Maine was never federal public domain. Until 1820 it was the District of Maine, part of Massachusetts, and the Commonwealth disposed of its "eastern lands" itself: through a Committee for the Sale of Eastern Lands from 1783 and a Land Office from 1801. In the interior the usual unit was the township, or half of one, sold to a buyer or granted to an academy, a college, a town or a body of settlers. When Maine became a state, the land still unsold was divided between Massachusetts and Maine, township by township, in a series of divisions beginning in 1822, and each state then sold its own share.

    The 135 entries shown here are present-day townships whose first title from the Commonwealth has a known year: 36 townships sold partly or wholly by the land lottery of 1787; 74 townships in the two tracts sold to William Bingham in 1793, 27 east of the Penobscot and 47 on the upper Kennebec; and 25 townships granted or sold by a resolve whose date has been found. They are shown under Maine, where the land lies, although Massachusetts issued nearly all of them. Each is one record, so Maine's count is of townships, not of people who received land, and it is not comparable with the patent counts of other states.

    Most of Maine is not yet on the map. Not shown are the older titles along the coast and the lower rivers (Crown and proprietary grants before 1692, Indian deeds, and the province's town grants); about 180 more townships that Massachusetts sold or granted between 1783 and 1829, whose dates have not yet been found; the townships assigned to Massachusetts and to Maine after 1820 and sold later; and the far north, which was still undivided in 1829.

    Which township fell in which class is read from Moses Greenleaf's 1829 Map exhibiting the principal original grants and sales of lands in the State of Maine, fitted to present-day township lines, so an entry stands for the present township, not the tract as first surveyed. The Kennebec tract is traced from that map and is drawn about a row of townships too wide. In the lottery townships the land not drawn in the lottery went to Bingham in 1793; they are shown once, at 1787. Dates of the 25 separate grants come from the Massachusetts acts and resolves and from plans in the Maine Land Office's first plan book; seven are marked probable, and several date only one academy's half of a township. No acreage total is shown for the state. The holders are not in the name search or the Surname Origin view. All Maine entries are counted as grants and claims.

    Ohio's older surveys

    Ohio was where the federal survey was worked out, and much of the state was laid off before the standard grid existed: the Seven Ranges and the Ohio River Survey in the south-east, the lands between and west of the Miami rivers, and the United States Military District in the centre. Their townships are numbered from rivers and private purchase lines, so the method used for the other states could not place them, and until November 2026 they were missing from the map. 85,756 Ohio patents on these surveys are now shown. Each survey's grid was fitted so that its townships fall inside the county each patent names, and a patent is placed at the centre of its township, not at its own section. The list under a cell says so on each row. On the map this shows as stripes: a township's patents pile into one or two cells with thinner cells between.

    The Virginia Military District, between the Scioto and Little Miami rivers, was kept by Virginia to pay its Revolutionary soldiers and was never surveyed into townships. A warrant holder chose his own ground and had it run by metes and bounds, and the United States then issued the patent. The GLO index holds 4,775 of these patents, 2,625,405 acres, with no township, county or survey number. 273 have been placed on the stream named in the scanned patent. The rest are spread evenly over the district, so they show that the land was patented and to whom, but not where inside the district it lies. They are not in the Surname Origin view. Many early Virginia Military District patents are not in the GLO index at all.

    The Fire Lands, the western end of Connecticut's Western Reserve, never passed through a federal land office. Connecticut granted the half-million acres in May 1792 to the people burned out by British raids, and on 9 November 1808 the 120 quarter-township sections were drawn by lot among 120 groups of claims. The map shows that 1808 partition, counted under Ohio: 417 entries, one for each holder a section was classified to, as printed in W. W. Williams's History of the Fire-Lands (1879), spelled as printed. Each holder's acreage is a share of the section's mapped area in proportion to the value of the claims held there, so it is an estimate, and the mapped areas add up to 507,655 acres against the 500,000 granted. Most holders had bought their claims from the original sufferers. For Berlin, Ruggles and Danbury no table is printed and the sections are shown without holders. The sufferers themselves are on a separate page, The Fire Lands. Fire Lands entries are counted as grants and claims and are not in the Surname Origin view.

    Still blank or thin in Ohio: the rest of the Connecticut Western Reserve in the north-east, which Connecticut sold and whose first titles are land-company deeds; the Ohio Company's purchase around Marietta and John Cleves Symmes's purchase around Cincinnati, where the United States patented the whole tract to the company and the buyers took company deeds; and the canal, school and road lands the State of Ohio sold itself.

    Reading it carefully

    Year is the patent's signature date, or its search date where no signature date was recorded. That date marks when title passed, which can lag settlement by five years or more under the Homestead Act.

    Entry types are grouped from the GLO "authority" field. Homestead covers original, enlarged and stock-raising entries; cash sale includes credit sales; warrant and scrip covers military bounty warrants and scrip; grants and claims covers state grants, railroad and Indian patents, and private land claims. Non-patent records are BLM land-status entries (CDI, survey plats and LSR serial records) that sit alongside patents in the GLO database.

    Completeness: the site holds 5.9 million dated patents, 99.5% or more of what the GLO search reports for each state except Alaska (86%) and Ohio (99.2%). An earlier download had stopped at the search's 100,000-record limit in 25 states; those gaps were filled in November 2026, along with the land-status (CDI) and survey records, which are now 99.8% complete or better.

    What's missing: records without any usable date (about 190,000 location rows, concentrated in Alaska and New Mexico), locations under unspecified meridians, and some special surveys that do not fit the rectangular grid. The original thirteen states were never federal public domain and have no GLO patents. Texas wasn't federal land either, but it kept and granted its own; see Texas above. North Carolina is the first of the thirteen to be added, from its own state records, Maryland the second, Vermont the third, Rhode Island the fourth Virginia the fifth, bringing West Virginia and Kentucky with it, and Connecticut the sixth. Georgia follows, then New Hampshire and Massachusetts. Maine, which was part of Massachusetts until 1820, is here too; see North Carolina, Maryland, Vermont, Rhode Island, Virginia, Connecticut, Georgia, New Hampshire, Massachusetts and Maine above.
